More Than the Air We Breathe

The clouds drifting overhead, the wind moving the trees, the sunshine warming your face — all of it is a product of Earth's atmosphere. It stretches from the surface up to as far as 10,000 kilometers (6,214 miles) above, after which it simply blends into space. Yet the bulk of it sits close to home: most of the air we live in is packed into the first eight to 15 kilometers (5 to 9 miles) above the ground.

Earth's atmosphere is so much more than the air we breathe.

What the Air Is Made Of

Though oxygen is essential for most life on Earth, it is not the primary component of our air. The atmosphere is a mostly nitrogen blanket with a few crucial trace gases mixed in, and even the small amounts matter: carbon dioxide, methane, and water vapor shape our climate, while argon and noble gases round out the mix.

Five Layers, One Blanket

Scientists divide the atmosphere into five layers based on how temperature changes with altitude. The lowest, the troposphere, is the thickest at the equator and is where nearly all weather happens. Above it, the stratosphere holds the ozone layer that soaks up harmful ultraviolet light, while the higher layers grow thin and strange — home to auroras, meteors, and the satellites that orbit just above them.

  • Troposphere — 7 to 15 km up, where clouds and weather live
  • Stratosphere — home of the ozone layer
  • Mesosphere — where most meteors burn up
  • Thermosphere — auroras glow here
  • Exosphere — the fading edge that blends into space

How We Learned to Look Up

Before planes and rockets, studying the sky meant going up by foot, by balloon, or by sheer stubbornness. Each milestone below pushed the boundary of how high humans could measure the air.

  1. 1648Blaise Pascal and Florin Perier carry a meter-tall mercury barometer up the Puy de Dôme volcano in France, proving air pressure falls with altitude
  2. 1770sOzone is discovered independently by Priestley and Scheele
  3. 1787Horace-Bénédict de Saussure climbs Mont Blanc taking atmospheric measurements
  4. 1902Richard Assmann's high-altitude findings reveal the stratosphere
  5. 1957The International Geophysical Year expands global research on atmospheric ozone

Why the Blanket Matters

Earth's atmosphere protects the planet in more ways than one. It shields life from the sun's heat and radiation, holds the air we breathe, and drives the weather and climate systems that make the planet habitable. This thin band of air is unique to Earth — and without it, life as we know it would not exist.
